You look up what to do instead of scrolling and get the usual list: go for a walk, meditate, read a book, call someone.

All of them leave your hands free.

**Scrolling happens in your hands.** Thumb, wrist, screen, hundreds of times a day. What really competes for that spot is something else to do with your hands, and that's why walks and podcasts fail so often: people end up doing them with the phone out.

There's a second reason "just read" doesn't work at eleven at night. A book asks for the attention that scrolling already used up. Crafts ask for your hands and a softer kind of attention, which is a different resource and is often still there when the other one has run out.

There is reasonable evidence that this helps, with a limit worth saying out loud: most of it is self-reported. In a study of women hospitalized with anorexia nervosa, **74% said knitting distracted them or put distance** between them and the thoughts that occupied them, and more than half felt less likely to act on those thoughts.[^knitting] It's a specific group, and these are perceptions, not objective measures. Still, the research on crafts and wellbeing points the same way.[^review]

## You can start these in under two minutes

If starting takes more effort than opening Instagram, you'll open Instagram. That's about friction more than willpower.

### 1. A half-finished puzzle on the table

The trick is that it's already started and in plain sight. Five pieces count.

### 2. An open notebook with a pen on top

Writing by hand keeps both hands busy and moves at the speed of your thoughts, not the speed of the screen. It doesn't have to be a journal. A grocery list works.

### 3. Shuffling cards and playing solitaire

With real cards. Shuffling is half the point.

### 4. Watering and checking on your plants

Two minutes, busy hands, and you have to look closely.

### 5. Peeling something

Garlic for the week, oranges, a bag of pistachios. It sounds silly, and it works for the same reason as everything else here.

## They take a while, but no skill

### 6. Kneading bread

Ten minutes of fully busy hands, then hours where the dough does the work. If you keep sourdough starter in the fridge, it reminds you on its own.

### 7. Watercolor painting

It's cheap and dries fast, and you don't need a plan. The water also makes you wait between layers, and waiting is something the feed never lets you do.

### 8. Sewing a button, a hem or a torn seam

Almost everyone has a piece of clothing waiting. It's the only one on this list that also gets something off your to-do list.

### 9. Knitting or crochet

The most studied of all of them. It's repetitive by design, and the repetition creates a soft kind of focus, close to what you're looking for when you open your phone without meaning to.

### 10. Drawing whatever is in front of you

A glass, your hand, the window. It doesn't have to look good. Drawing makes you look at something slowly, for minutes at a time.

### 11. An instrument, even if you play it badly

Fifteen minutes of scales keep your hands, ears and attention busy at once. There isn't much attention left over for the phone.

## They leave something behind when you're done

### 12. Fixing something that's broken

A drawer that won't close, a frayed cable, a wobbly chair. There's a specific kind of satisfaction in getting something working again.

### 13. Cooking something slow

Something with several steps, like a stew or a sauce. The waiting is when you used to reach for your phone, and now you have something to check on.

### 14. Writing a letter by hand

To a real person, with an envelope and a stamp. It takes a while, and nothing pings while you write it.

### 15. Clearing out one whole drawer

Just one drawer. You can finish it in twenty minutes, and the feed never lets you finish anything.

## If the problem is the blank page

Three things on this list (writing, drawing and painting) come with a trap. You tell yourself you're going to do it, you open the notebook, and there's the empty page. It discourages you faster than your thumb can get back to the phone.

So here are some prompts. Pick one without thinking too hard, because choosing is tiring too.

### For your journal

None of these ask "how are you?". That question is too open, and at eleven at night it tends to send you in circles. Each of these is tied to something concrete from the day you just had.

1. Something you saw today and didn't tell anyone.
2. Which hour of the day was really yours.
3. The first thing you did today that nobody asked you to do.
4. Something you were going to say and didn't.
5. What was harder today than it should have been.
6. Something someone else did today that helped you.
7. What would have been harder today if that person weren't around.
8. Something that worked and that you only notice when it breaks.
9. What you'd do tomorrow if nobody was going to ask you about it.

The middle three are about gratitude, even though they don't use the word. Gratitude writing has more evidence behind it than any other journaling technique, and it still works better when it isn't asked in the abstract. "What are you grateful for?" gets autopilot answers like family, health or coffee. And at eleven at night, asking an exhausted person to be grateful can sound like you're telling them to complain less.

The seventh and eighth ask it the other way around, by imagining something missing instead of thanking it. They're harder to answer on autopilot.

### For creative writing

These aren't about you, and sometimes that's exactly what you need.

10. Write the last sentence of a story that doesn't exist.
11. Describe a room by what it sounds like, not by what it looks like.
12. Two people are waiting for the same bus. One of them is lying.
13. An object in your home tells how it got there.
14. Someone goes back to a place after ten years, and only one thing has changed.

### For drawing or painting

15. The exact color of the light in the room right now.
16. An object in your home, without lifting your pencil from the paper.
17. What was outside your window an hour ago, from memory.
18. The same mug three times, faster each time.
19. Only the shadows of something, without drawing the thing itself.

None of them have to turn out well. Number 16 always comes out wrong, and that's why it works: once it can't look good, there's no reason left to quit.

## What won't work, and why

**Swapping the scroll for another screen.** Shows, games and videos take your attention but not your hands, and the free hand finds the phone. Just look at how many people scroll while they watch a show.

**Picking something that needs a whole setup.** If you have to pull things out of a closet, it won't happen at eleven at night.

**Promising yourself an hour.** Scrolling wins because it costs nothing to start, so whatever you pick has to be almost as easy to begin. Ten minutes is plenty.

## And one thing that isn't an activity

Sometimes you didn't want to do anything. You wanted a little while where nobody needed anything from you, and the phone was the only thing around.

A list won't fix that. What fixes it is getting that time some other way, and none of these fifteen will help if the real problem was that you didn't have a single moment to yourself all day.
