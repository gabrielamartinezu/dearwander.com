You get to the end of the day and you've seen a thousand things. A war and a puppy. Forty photos of other people's dinners. Three arguments you never joined, a recipe you'll never cook, someone's engagement, someone's breakdown. Your eyes are tired in that particular way. And nothing came out of you all day. Not one thing you made, wrote, drew, or built.

That quiet hollowness has less to do with discipline than with a ratio. We spend the whole day taking things in, and almost nothing goes back out.

Consuming was never the enemy. People have always read, watched, and listened, and been made larger by it. A feed is just consumption stripped down to its purest form: input with no output, forever. And there's now a good deal of research suggesting that this particular kind of intake sits badly with us. A [2024 meta-analysis of 141 studies](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/1/zmad055/7595758) found passive social media use, the scrolling-without-making kind, tied to worse wellbeing across the board. A related study on [mindless scrolling](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/29/1/zmad056/7582213) found it comes wrapped in guilt and a low background hum of conflict with your own goals. The bad feeling you have at the end of a long scroll is documented. You didn't imagine it.

## What making does that consuming can't

Here's the part worth sitting with. The opposite of that hollow feeling turns out to be measurable too, and it's smaller and more ordinary than "become an artist."

Researchers Tamlin Conner, Colin DeYoung, and Paul Silvia followed 658 young adults for thirteen days, asking each night what they'd done, what they'd made, and how they felt. They published the result in 2018 as [*Everyday creative activity as a path to flourishing*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2016.1257049). On days people did more creative activity than usual, they woke the next day with more energy, more positive emotion, and a stronger sense of flourishing.

The direction matters. The lift showed up the day *after* the making, which points to the creating causing the good feeling rather than happy people simply making more. A small act of making on Tuesday left a residue that was still there on Wednesday.

Notice how modest their bar for "creative" was. Not a gallery show. Cooking something new, writing, sketching, arranging, fixing. Anything where something existed at the end that hadn't existed at the start.

## You don't need to be good at it

The obvious objection is that this is fine for people who can actually draw. The research says otherwise, and it's specific.

At Drexel University, Girija Kaimal and her colleagues had people make art for [forty-five minutes and measured their cortisol](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2016/june/art_hormone_levels_lower), the hormone your body pushes out under stress. In most of them, it dropped. What predicted the drop was not talent or past experience. Someone who'd never touched a marker got the same settling as someone who paints.

It's a small study, and it didn't work for everyone. A few people's stress ticked up, probably the ones telling themselves the whole time that they were doing it wrong. So hold it lightly. But the shape of the finding is a gift: the good part of making things was never reserved for the gifted. It lives in the doing.

You can watch this play out beyond a lab. When Jill Riley, Betsan Corkhill, and Clare Morris surveyed [more than 3,500 knitters in 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.4276/030802213X13603244419077), over eighty percent said they felt happier after knitting. The word that kept surfacing was flow, that state where the clock disappears and your hands and your mind move together without friction. A feed is engineered to hold your attention. Flow is what it feels like to give it away freely, and to want to.

## The trap: making things for the feed

There's one distinction that decides whether any of this works, and it's easy to miss.

The moment you start making *in order to post* is the moment you quietly hand the whole thing back to the feed. Now the drawing needs to be good enough to share. Now the loaf has to photograph well. The making stops being yours and becomes content, which is just consumption wearing a maker's apron. You're back to performing for the same scroll, only from the other side of the glass.

Try making something no one will ever see. A bad drawing in a cheap notebook. A photo you take and never post. A song you hum into your phone's voice memos and delete. The uselessness is the point. A thing made only for you can't be liked, ranked, or lost to the algorithm, and that's exactly what makes it restful.

This is also why [a screen time app that shames you](/blog/why-screen-time-apps-shouldnt-punish-you/) tends to backfire. Guilt is just another feed, another thing to consume about yourself. The gentler move is to add something good rather than only subtract something bad.

## How to tip the ratio

You're only trying to move the daily balance a few degrees, so that a little more comes out of you than used to go in.

Start absurdly small. Five minutes. If you draw on an iPad, open Procreate and copy one object on your desk badly. A mug, a plant, your own hand. If you'd rather stay off the screen, a pencil and the back of an envelope work the same. The [things worth studying](/blog/things-to-study-instead-of-scrolling/) make good subjects, since looking closely enough to draw something is its own kind of attention.

Put it where the scroll usually happens. The ten minutes in bed, the queue, the coffee going cold. Those are the exact slots the feed has claimed, and they're the easiest to reclaim, because your hands are already free and looking for something to do.

Keep it useless on purpose, something with no audience and no streak riding on it. What's left is the small, real satisfaction of having made a thing today, which is the one thing an hour of scrolling never leaves you.

Dear Wander is built around that same small hinge: a breath between the reach for your phone and the tap, long enough to ask what you actually want to do with the next ten minutes. Sometimes the honest answer is to keep scrolling, and that's allowed. But more often than you'd expect, given the pause, you'll reach for the pencil instead.

Make a little more than you consume. Only a little, just enough that at the end of the day, something exists that came from you.
